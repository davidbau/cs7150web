Summary

In this course we will learn the principles and practice of deep learning methods and research.

We will cover the capabilities of deep networks, the main methods for training them effectively, and the common architectures and techniques for using a deep network to process and produce images and natural language text. In addition to getting experience using these methods, we will discuss seminal research papers, overview some of the main questions and debates that have emerged in deep learning research, and explore some current research topics. There will be a final project where you work with a small team to choose, investigate, and explain a deep learning research topic centered on a paper you choose.

How this course works

This course is about three things: learning to learn, learning to teach, and teaching to learn.

Learning to learn: picking up a tutorial or an unfamiliar paper, working out what it teaches, and learning to build what it describes. That is what the homeworks and exams are for. This semester there will be five homework assignments, each scored by an in-class homework quiz, and one midterm exam.

Learning to teach: explaining is a technical skill, and researchers spend much of their time on it. Papers, talks, diagrams, demonstrations and reviews are all ways of doing it, and you will practise all five. You will read 19 seminal papers and submit a discussion-sparking question for each one, and present one of those papers in class, creating an explanation and interactive visualization with a small team.

Teaching to learn: you will create a final project with a small team, and you will present your own work the same way you presented a seminal paper, providing context, technical explanations, and an interactive visualization explaining what you have learned.

The course is graded in a way to encourage you to do good work in the course, to keep learning even when things are hard, so that one bad day does not follow you around.

This course has been restructured for the LLM assistance era. LLM assistance is allowed and expected, but you must use it to enhance your understanding and your ability to explain the concepts of this course to your peers. Almost no points in the course are granted for work that an LLM can do (only 5/205 points are for handing in homework); your goal is not just to produce some working code, but to learn the material well enough to work with the concepts on your own in quizzes and exams, to explain important concepts to your peers, and to gain insights and explain what you have learned when doing your group project.

Student-Led Paper Discussions

Class participation and paper presentations: 50 points. In-class paper presentations and discussion.

Semester Overview: The centerpiece of the course is a series of student-led discussions. Throughout the semester, you will read and we will discuss 19 seminal research papers. Thirteen of those lectures will begin with a student-led discussion of one of the selected papers. Each of those student-led discussions is led by a panel of four students, who take four different roles, with follow-up questions from the rest of the class (the questioners). (With four to a panel everybody has a distinct job and time to speak.) You will present on exactly one panel during the semester, so the rest of your participation grade comes from submitting nightly reading questions and from discussion that your questions spark. These papers and their respective dates are pre-scheduled & can be seen in timeline schedule of the course structure.

Points Breakdown & To Dos (50 Points):

  1. Nightly Questions: The night before each class that has an assigned paper, read the paper and submit a question on Canvas, with a sentence on why you think it matters. There are 19 such classes, 13 led by student panels. Prof. Bau will curate questions that students submit to drive the discussion by the panel, so when you submit a nightly question it is a way to put a question to both the class and Prof. Bau. Each question earns 1 point, for a total of 19 Points. In several of the classes, a second paper is labeled also see, which you are also encouraged to skim. Submit reading questions about whatever you actually want to know. The main reading is the obvious place to start, but a related paper, the connection to other research or industry work, something from an earlier week, or something from outside the syllabus entirely are all fine. It is one question per class day, not one per paper. The nightly questions are part of your participation grade, so do not skip them.
  2. In-Class Interactions: Prof. Bau will call students during any lecture, including asking you to present your nightly question, or to respond to a discussion in class; all together, class participation is worth 10 points.
  3. Paper Presentation & Review: Once during the semester you will be assigned one of the four roles below, to review and present one aspect of a paper, which will let you read that paper from a particular angle. Your paper presentation is a collaboration with three other students on your panel; your team will need to create an interactive visualization and a slide deck to spark the discussion (more on that below). Leading this discussion well earns up to 21 Points.

Each student works with a group to help lead the discussion of one paper during the term, playing one of four roles on a team of four.

The four roles (one student each), in the order they present:

  1. Archaeologist: Give the historical context and the biography of the authors. Explain the ideas that came before this paper, and what problem it was answering, and what intellectual impact it had after it was published. Explain the biography of the authors: who wrote it, where they were and who they were working with at the time, what they had done before, and what they went on to do.
  2. Diagrammer: Create a slide that visually explains the method of the paper. And use it to explain the technical meat of the paper. What do the key formulas mean? How does the architecture work? What should you take away from the paper's findings?
  3. Demo Presenter: Present a working technical artifact that teaches the paper's central idea, and run it live for the class. The whole panel builds the demonstration together; the demo presenter is the one who presents it, which means you have to understand it well enough to be asked about it. Details below.
  4. Reviewer: Address the standard NeurIPS review questions about the paper. Write a real peer review (put the review document in the shared Drive folder), with suggestions to the authors on what seemed weak or strong to you about the paper. Put a summary of your review on your slide. You go last, and your review opens the discussion.

Panels that are not exactly four. Depending on enrollment, a few panels will be uneven. A panel of five puts a second student on the demonstration. A panel of three merges the Archaeologist and the Reviewer into one role, the Academician, who opens the session with the context and closes it with the review.

Format & Preparation:

- Duration: Each paper discussion will last 45 minutes

- Roles & Sign-up: Fill in the role-playing sign-up form on Canvas before September 15, 2026. Rank the papers you would most like to present and the roles you would prefer. We will try to honor your preferences, but with one panel per student some adjustment is inevitable.

- Demonstration (built by the whole panel, presented by one): Every panel must bring a short interactive demonstration of the paper's central idea — ideally something with a slider or a text box, so the class can change an input and watch what happens. A Colab notebook is the usual form. Building it is the panel's shared job: all four of you should have run it and told the Demo Presenter what was confusing. The Demo Presenter runs it in front of the room and answers for it. The aim is not a reproduction of the paper's results. It is one accurate, live illustration of the mechanism, small enough to run in the room, that leaves the class understanding something they did not understand before.

Use an LLM coding assistant to build it, and say so.

You are graded on what you chose to show — which mechanism from the paper you picked, what a viewer can change, and what they see when they change it — and on whether the demonstration is accurate and informative. Accurate: it really does implement what the paper describes, so check it against the paper before you show it. Informative: someone who watches it comes away understanding the mechanism better than they did. A demo that gets either of those wrong teaches the class the wrong thing.

On the demonstration slide, say briefly what the panel asked the assistant for and what you had to fix.

Some papers are about phenomena that only appear at a scale you cannot run in class. For those, demonstrate a scaled-down analogue, or make the paper's own figures interactive — let the class move along a curve the authors published. If you are not sure what would work for your paper, ask early.

- Slide Deck & Materials Submission: The panel submits its materials one night before the scheduled discussion. This includes:

- The Google Slides deck, with a slide from each of the four panelists (go to the shared drive and read the 'INSTRUCTIONS: For reference purposes' file, which says what each role should prepare and where to upload it)

- The panel's interactive demonstration, as a link that runs from a fresh session on somebody else's computer. We will post it for the class, so anyone can open it and play with it afterwards. A Colab notebook is the usual form; a hosted page is fine too.

- The Reviewer's detailed review, uploaded to the shared Drive folder

- A question about the paper for the broader class (submitted as your nightly question on Canvas), and an acknowledgment of which role you are playing

Suggested demonstrations, by paper. These are ideas we think could be insightful, not requirements — if you have a better idea for your paper, propose it. Each one gives the class something to change and something to watch happen.

PaperAn interactive visualization that could be insightful
Kingma 2015 (ADAM)A contour plot of an ill-conditioned 2-D loss such as 10x2 + y2, with the optimizer's path drawn on it. Sliders for learning rate, beta-1 and beta-2, and a switch between plain SGD, momentum, RMSProp and ADAM. A second panel showing the running m and v for each coordinate. The insight: why per-coordinate normalization straightens out the zig-zag, and how large a learning rate ADAM survives before it diverges.
Glorot 2010 (initialization)A 30-layer MLP with a slider for the initial weight scale and a dropdown for tanh, sigmoid or ReLU. Show the activation histogram at every layer as a strip across depth, plus the gradient norm per layer. The insight: slide the scale and watch activations collapse toward zero or saturate, and see that the Xavier and Kaiming values are exactly where the variance stays flat with depth.
He 2016 (ResNet)Two networks side by side, plain and residual, with a depth slider from 2 to 60 layers. Plot the gradient norm arriving at each layer, and a short training curve for each. The insight: the plain network's gradient decays geometrically with depth while the residual one stays flat, and the plain network's training error gets worse as it gets deeper, which is the paper's actual headline.
Cho 2014 (GRUs)A single GRU cell with sliders for the input, the previous hidden state, and the reset and update gate pre-activations, showing r, z, the candidate and the new h as bars. Then a long sequence with one thing that has to be remembered, and a slider on the update-gate bias. The insight: how many steps a memory survives as you open and close the gate. (HW3 has a widget close to this to start from.)
Vaswani 2017 (Transformers)Type a sentence and see the attention matrix of a small pretrained model, per head and layer; click a token to see what it attends to. A switch to zero out the positional embeddings. The insight: different heads doing visibly different jobs, and the structure collapsing when position information is removed.
Radford 2021 (CLIP)A text box for candidate labels and a handful of images, with the image-text similarity matrix computed live, so the class can invent new labels and watch zero-shot classification change. Then break it: try a typographic attack, writing a word onto an image. The insight: one shared embedding space for both modalities, and how shallow its grip on the image can be.
Goodfellow 2014 (GAN)A 2-D target such as two moons. Show the generator's samples over the discriminator's decision surface as a background heatmap, updating as it trains, with sliders for the D-to-G update ratio and the two learning rates. The insight: the boundary chasing the samples, and mode collapse induced on purpose by over-training the discriminator.
Kingma 2013 (VAE)A VAE with a 2-D latent trained on MNIST. A draggable pad over the latent plane that decodes to an image live, plus a beta slider. The insight: the latent space as a map you can walk around, and what raising beta does to both reconstruction sharpness and how fully the prior's mass decodes to something plausible.
Dinh 2017 (Real NVP)A 2-D density warped by coupling layers, with a slider for how many layers have been applied and a toggle for the mask pattern, overlaying the running log-determinant. The insight: an invertible warp building up one layer at a time, and what goes wrong when you stop alternating the mask, so one coordinate is never transformed.
Sohl-Dickstein 2015 (Diffusion)A single t slider from 0 to T: forward noising on the left, the reverse trajectory from a trained model on the right, and the alpha-bar schedule plotted underneath with the current t marked. The insight: the two processes as mirror images, and the schedule as the object that ties them together.
Brown 2020 (GPT-3)Few-shot prompting with a small local model. A box where you add in-context examples one at a time, with accuracy on a small task plotted live against the number of examples. Alongside it, make the paper's own scaling curves interactive: slide model size and watch the zero-shot and few-shot gap. This is one of the two papers whose real phenomenon needs more compute than a classroom has, so the right move is a scaled-down analogue plus the authors' published data.
Rafailov 2023 (DPO)A small preference sandbox. Start with a handful of candidate responses and pairwise preferences over them, and fit a Bradley-Terry reward model live. Then show the same preferences trained directly into a policy by the DPO loss, and plot the DPO implicit reward (β log π/πref) against the explicitly fitted one. The insight: the two rankings agreeing even though no reward model was ever trained in the second case, and then the β slider pulling the policy away from the reference until it starts to hack the preferences. Like GPT-3, this needs a toy scale to stay accurate.
DeepSeek 2025 (R1, RLVR)Build on HW5.1's own tiny GRPO loop: sliders for the KL coefficient, the length penalty and the group size, plotting reward, the fraction of scratchpad-style answers, and a live sample of the model's outputs. The insight: the reward-hacking result from the homework, reproduced in front of the room.

Class Procedure (during presentation):

  2. Diagrammer (2-3 mins): the method itself, on one picture.
  3. Demo Presenter (5-10 mins): run the demonstration live, and take a suggestion from the class for what to change.
  4. Reviewer (2-3 mins): what is and is not established by the paper.
  5. Open discussion (the rest): Prof. Bau or TAs will select students in sequence to pose their previously submitted questions to the panel.

Homework and Quizzes

Homework hand-in: 5 points. Programming and calculation exercises.

There are five homeworks, jupyter notebooks to be worked through by students individually. In this age of LLM assistance, we will not be scoring your homework code in detail. Instead, each completed homework you hand in on Canvas earns 1 point, for a total of 5.

There is no deadline on this and no late penalty. Hand a homework in whenever it is done, at any point in the semester — including before it is assigned, if you want to work ahead. The dates in the schedule above are when each homework is meant to be done, because that is what the quizzes are timed against, but the point does not depend on them. There are no late days to keep track of. Suggestion: hand in all the homeworks early.

Homework quizzes: 50 points. Five short quizzes, 10 points each, taken in class.

There is one homework quiz per homework assignment, and each quiz is given in class on the day that homework is due (the dates are in the schedule above). Those days are deliberately days without a student paper presentation, so that the class is not carrying two things at once. Each quiz tests your knowledge of the methods and concepts in that homework. The quizzes are designed to be much easier for you if you have actually worked through and understood the homework yourself, so the best preparation for a quiz is to do the homework yourself. This is where the real incentive to keep up with the homework lies: handing the homework in is worth 1 point, but its quiz is worth 10.

Quiz 3 is on October 20, two days before the midterm. HW3 covers recurrent networks, gating and attention, which are on the midterm, so doing it is part of preparing for the exam. That class is the midterm review, and the discussion before the quiz is where to bring your questions.

Quiz 1 is on September 17, before the September 22 add/drop deadline. HW1 is short and goes out on the first day, so by the deadline you will have done one homework and one quiz. Use them to decide whether to stay in the course.

On each of those five days we will open the class with about ten minutes of informal discussion of the homework, before the quiz. Bring your questions. If that discussion happens to give away the answer to something on the quiz, so be it — that is a reward for being in the room and paying attention.

Quizzes must be taken in person, in class on their scheduled day, to earn points. There is no remote or online version.

Each quiz is worth 10 points, and whether a quiz counts is decided as follows:

  1. A quiz that would pull your grade down is dropped — out of the course total as well as out of your points — so a quiz that goes badly does not count against you.
  2. A quiz you skip counts as a zero, and that zero stays in the total.
  3. Coming to a make-up drops the missed quiz instead, the same way a low quiz you sat would be dropped.

The reason for scoring quizzes this way is that we would much rather you came to class than stayed away worried about a quiz. Come and take it.

For example: if you sit all five quizzes and they all help you, your grade is out of the full 205. If two of them would drag you down, those two are dropped and your grade is out of 185. If you sat none of them but came to all five make-up discussions, all fifty quiz points would drop out and your grade would be computed out of 155.

Make-ups run for 14 days after the quiz. A make-up begins with a second sitting of the quiz, and is then graded together with a short discussion of that homework with a TA, rather than marked cold — so if there is something you did not follow, that conversation is the place to sort it out. Come to office hours or arrange a time with a TA. After 14 days the make-up closes and the zero stands. If you know in advance that you will miss a quiz, tell us before the date rather than after.

The catch is that the quizzes must be done in-person in class. Each homework quiz is given in class on its scheduled day whether or not you have handed in the homework yet. The quizzes are worth 50 points and the homework hand-ins are worth 5, so the main reason to do a homework on time is not to hand it in: the homework is the best way to learn the material for the homework quiz. If you have done the homework yourself and understand the concepts, the quiz should be much easier.

Midterm Exam

Midterm exam: 50 points. A written, closed-book exam about foundational methods, held in class. The starred items in the concept list below are the ones that may appear on it.

Final Project Presentations

Final Project: 50 points. An illustrated blog or webpage report plus an in-class presentation, about a research topic centered on a paper you choose. Done in teams of 2 or 3 students.

This is the paper presentation again, except that you choose the paper and your team plays all four roles. Your project must give the context for the work, a technical explanation of the method and what its key formulas and architecture mean, an assessment of what the paper does and does not establish, and an interactive visualization of the central idea. You may also include a research extension of the concepts in the paper you choose.

The interactive visualization is required, not optional. The same standard applies as for the paper demonstrations: it should be accurate and informative, and a reader should be able to change something and see what happens. Hand it in as a link that runs from a fresh session on somebody else's computer, and we will post it for the class so everyone can open it and play with it at their own machine. A Colab notebook is the usual form; a hosted page is fine too. The measure of the whole project is whether a reader finishes it understanding the topic better than they did.

Collaboration Policy

Collaboration is allowed, but you should think about the problems yourself before discussing them with others or having an LLM solve it for you. When you do seek out help, we strongly advise you to find a fellow classmate to talk with and work together rather than just copying an answer. You will all learn much more by thinking collaboratively and explaining ideas to one another.
