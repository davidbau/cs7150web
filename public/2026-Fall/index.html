Summary

In this course we will learn the principles and practice of deep learning methods and research.

We will cover the capabilities of deep networks, the main methods for training them effectively, and the common architectures and techniques for using a deep network to process and produce images and natural language text.  In addition to getting experience using these methods, we will discuss seminal research papers, overview some of the main questions and debates that have emerged in deep learning research, and explore some current research topics.  There will be a final project where you work with a partner to choose, investigate, and write a blog exploration of a deep learning research topic centered on a paper you choose.

How this course works

This course is not really about collecting points.  It is about three things: learning to learn, learning to teach, and teaching to learn.

Learning to learn, because the field moves faster than any syllabus.  Most of what you will need in five years does not exist yet, so the durable skill is being able to pick up an unfamiliar paper, work out what it actually claims, and build the thing it describes.  That is what the readings and the homework are for.

Learning to teach, because explaining is a technical skill and it is most of what a researcher does.  Papers, talks, diagrams, demonstrations and reviews are all attempts to move an idea into somebody else's head, and they mostly fail.  You will practise all five.

Teaching to learn, because this is the fastest way to find out what you do not know.  An idea you cannot make legible to another person is an idea you do not yet have.  Preparing a slide, a demonstration or a review will expose the gaps in your own understanding faster than any amount of re-reading, which is why so much of the course is built out of explaining things to each other rather than listening to me.

The points below exist to make the mechanics fair and predictable, not to be the object of the exercise.  They are arranged so that doing the work well and scoring well point in the same direction, and so that a bad day does not follow you around.  If you find yourself optimizing the scoring rather than the learning, something has gone wrong and I would rather hear about it than have you play the game.

One rule follows directly from all this, and it explains an apparent contradiction later on: sometimes this course tells you to use an LLM coding assistant and sometimes it tells you not to lean on one.  The line is the same in both cases.  Where the goal is to build something for other people to learn from, use the best tools you have and say what you used.  Where the goal is for you to understand a mechanism, do the thinking yourself — the quizzes are measuring you, not your assistant, and they are written to tell the difference.

Class participation and paper presentations: 50 points.  In-class paper presentations and discussion.

Semester Overview: Throughout the semester, 13 lectures will be dedicated to discussing each one of the 13 selected research papers.  Each paper is led by a panel of four students, who take four different roles, with follow-up questions from the rest of the class (the questioners).  With four to a panel everybody has a distinct job and real time to speak.  You will be on exactly one panel during the semester, so the rest of your participation grade comes from the reading questions and from discussion. These papers & their respective dates are pre-scheduled & can be seen in timeline schedule of the course structure.

Points Breakdown & To Dos (50 Points):

  1. Nightly Questions: The night before each class that has an assigned paper, read the paper and submit a question on Canvas, with a sentence on why you think it matters.  There are 19 such classes: the 13 led by student panels, and 6 more where Prof. Bau presents the paper himself (Rumelhart, Baydin, LeCun, Elman, Vaswani and Shao).  Prof. Bau reads the questions and curates them: the ones a panel is well placed to answer go to the panel, and he keeps some for himself, so a nightly question is also a way to put something to the instructor directly.  Each question earns 1 point, for a total of 19 Points.  A few days also list a second paper marked also skim.  Everyone is expected to skim those too, closely enough to say what the paper claims and how it did it; Prof. Bau will lecture on them to set up the main reading, and they are fair game in class and on a quiz.  Ask about whatever you actually want to know.  The main reading is the obvious place to start, but a skim paper, the connection between the two, something from an earlier week, or something from outside the syllabus entirely are all fine.  It is one question per class day, not one per paper, and a genuine question always beats a dutiful one.  This is the part of your participation grade you work at all semester, so do not skip it.
  2. In-Class Interactions: Prof. Bau may randomly call on students during any lecture, be it a paper presentation or a standard class. Students earn points from being called on and from other active participation, including asking your question of the panel or of Prof. Bau; this is worth 10 points.
  3. Paper Presentation & Review: Once during the semester you will be assigned one of the four roles below, to review and present one aspect of a paper, which will let you read that paper from a particular angle. This is collaborative work with the three other students on your panel, and requires a slide deck (more on that below). Leading this discussion well earns up to 21 Points.

The four roles (one student each), in the order they present:

  1. Archaeologist: Give the historical context and the biography of the authors.  The context: what came before this paper, and what problem it was answering.  The biography: who wrote it, where they were and who they were working with at the time, what they had done before, and what they went on to do.
  2. Diagrammer: Create a slide that visually explains the method of the paper.
  3. Demo Presenter: Present a working technical artifact that teaches the paper's central idea, and run it live for the class.  The whole panel builds the demonstration together; you are the one who presents it, which means you have to understand it well enough to be asked about it.  Details below.
  4. Reviewer: Address the NeurIPS review questions. Put a summary on your slide and upload the detailed review to the shared Drive folder.  You go last, and your review opens the discussion.

Panels that are not exactly four.  Depending on enrollment, a few panels will be uneven.  A panel of five puts a second student on the demonstration.  A panel of three merges the Archaeologist and the Reviewer into one role, the Academician, who opens the session with the context and closes it with the review.

Format & Preparation:

- Duration: Each paper discussion will last 45 minutes

- Roles & Sign-up: Fill in the role-playing sign-up form on Canvas before September 15, 2026. Rank the papers you would most like to present and the roles you would prefer. We will try to honor your preferences, but with one panel per student some adjustment is inevitable.

- Demonstration (built by the whole panel, presented by one): Every panel must bring a short interactive demonstration of the paper's central idea — ideally something with a slider or a text box, so the class can change an input and watch what happens. A Colab notebook is the usual form. Building it is the panel's shared job: all four of you should have run it and told the Demo Presenter what was confusing. The Demo Presenter runs it in front of the room and answers for it. The aim is not a reproduction of the paper's results. It is one accurate, live illustration of the mechanism, small enough to run in the room, that leaves the class understanding something they did not understand before.

  Use an LLM coding assistant to build it, and say so. This is the reverse of the homework rule; see “How this course works” above.

  You are graded on what you chose to show — which mechanism from the paper you picked, what a viewer can change, and what they see when they change it — and on whether the demonstration is accurate and informative. Accurate: it really does implement what the paper describes, so check it against the paper before you show it. Informative: someone who watches it comes away understanding the mechanism better than they did. A demo that gets either of those wrong teaches the class something false.

  On the demonstration slide, say briefly what the panel asked for and what you had to fix. Where the model got it wrong is usually the most interesting thing you will learn.

  Some papers are about phenomena that only appear at a scale you cannot run in class. For those, demonstrate a scaled-down analogue, or make the paper's own figures interactive — let the class move along a curve the authors published. If you are not sure what would work for your paper, ask early.

- Slide Deck & Materials Submission: The panel submits its materials one night before the scheduled discussion. This includes:

  - The Google Slides deck, with a slide from each of the four panelists (go to the shared drive and read the 'INSTRUCTIONS: For reference purposes' file, which says what each role should prepare and where to upload it)

  - The panel's interactive demonstration, linked and in a state that runs from a fresh session

  - The Reviewer's detailed review, uploaded to the shared Drive folder

  - A question about the paper for the broader class (submitted as your nightly question on Canvas), and an acknowledgment of which role you are playing

Suggested demonstrations, by paper.  These are ideas we think could be insightful, not requirements — if you have a better idea for your paper, propose it.  Each one gives the class something to change and something to watch happen.

PaperAn interactive visualization that could be insightful
Kingma 2015 (ADAM)A contour plot of an ill-conditioned 2-D loss such as 10x2 + y2, with the optimizer's path drawn on it. Sliders for learning rate, beta-1 and beta-2, and a switch between plain SGD, momentum, RMSProp and ADAM. A second panel showing the running m and v for each coordinate. The insight: why per-coordinate normalization straightens out the zig-zag, and how large a learning rate ADAM survives before it diverges.
Glorot 2010 (initialization)A 30-layer MLP with a slider for the initial weight scale and a dropdown for tanh, sigmoid or ReLU. Show the activation histogram at every layer as a strip across depth, plus the gradient norm per layer. The insight: slide the scale and watch activations collapse toward zero or saturate, and see that the Xavier and Kaiming values are exactly where the variance stays flat with depth.
He 2016 (ResNet)Two networks side by side, plain and residual, with a depth slider from 2 to 60 layers. Plot the gradient norm arriving at each layer, and a short training curve for each. The insight: the plain network's gradient decays geometrically with depth while the residual one stays flat, and the plain network's training error gets worse as it gets deeper, which is the paper's actual headline.
Cho 2014 (GRUs)A single GRU cell with sliders for the input, the previous hidden state, and the reset and update gate pre-activations, showing r, z, the candidate and the new h as bars. Then a long sequence with one thing that has to be remembered, and a slider on the update-gate bias. The insight: how many steps a memory survives as you open and close the gate. (HW3 has a widget close to this to start from.)
Vaswani 2017 (Transformers)Type a sentence and see the attention matrix of a small pretrained model, per head and layer; click a token to see what it attends to. A switch to zero out the positional embeddings. The insight: different heads doing visibly different jobs, and the structure collapsing when position information is removed.
Radford 2021 (CLIP)A text box for candidate labels and a handful of images, with the image-text similarity matrix computed live, so the class can invent new labels and watch zero-shot classification change. Then break it: try a typographic attack, writing a word onto an image. The insight: one shared embedding space for both modalities, and how shallow its grip on the image can be.
Goodfellow 2014 (GAN)A 2-D target such as two moons. Show the generator's samples over the discriminator's decision surface as a background heatmap, updating as it trains, with sliders for the D-to-G update ratio and the two learning rates. The insight: the boundary chasing the samples, and mode collapse induced on purpose by over-training the discriminator.
Kingma 2013 (VAE)A VAE with a 2-D latent trained on MNIST. A draggable pad over the latent plane that decodes to an image live, plus a beta slider. The insight: the latent space as a map you can walk around, and what raising beta does to both reconstruction sharpness and how fully the prior's mass decodes to something plausible.
Dinh 2017 (Real NVP)A 2-D density warped by coupling layers, with a slider for how many layers have been applied and a toggle for the mask pattern, overlaying the running log-determinant. The insight: an invertible warp building up one layer at a time, and what goes wrong when you stop alternating the mask, so one coordinate is never transformed.
Sohl-Dickstein 2015 (Diffusion)A single t slider from 0 to T: forward noising on the left, the reverse trajectory from a trained model on the right, and the alpha-bar schedule plotted underneath with the current t marked. The insight: the two processes as mirror images, and the schedule as the object that ties them together.
Brown 2020 (GPT-3)Few-shot prompting with a small local model. A box where you add in-context examples one at a time, with accuracy on a small task plotted live against the number of examples. Alongside it, make the paper's own scaling curves interactive: slide model size and watch the zero-shot and few-shot gap. This is one of the two papers whose real phenomenon needs more compute than a classroom has, so the right move is a scaled-down analogue plus the authors' published data.
Rafailov 2023 (DPO)A small preference sandbox. Start with a handful of candidate responses and pairwise preferences over them, and fit a Bradley-Terry reward model live. Then show the same preferences trained directly into a policy by the DPO loss, and plot the DPO implicit reward (β log π/πref) against the explicitly fitted one. The insight: the two rankings agreeing even though no reward model was ever trained in the second case, and then the β slider pulling the policy away from the reference until it starts to hack the preferences. Like GPT-3, this needs a toy scale to stay accurate.
Meng 2022 (ROME)A causal tracing grid. Take a factual statement, corrupt the subject tokens, then restore one layer's hidden state at one token position at a time and plot the recovered probability of the correct answer as a heatmap over layer and position. Then let the class edit a fact with a rank-one update and query the model before and after. The insight: the mid-layer peak at the subject tokens in the heatmap, and a single localized edit changing one answer while leaving unrelated facts alone.
DeepSeek 2025 (R1, RLVR)Build on HW5.1's own tiny GRPO loop: sliders for the KL coefficient, the length penalty and the group size, plotting reward, the fraction of scratchpad-style answers, and a live sample of the model's outputs. The insight: the reward-hacking result from the homework, reproduced in front of the room.

 Class Procedure (during presentation):

  1. Archaeologist (2-3 mins): where the paper came from and who wrote it.
  2. Diagrammer (2-3 mins): the method itself, on one picture.
  3. Demo Presenter (5-10 mins): run the demonstration live, and take a suggestion from the class for what to change.
  4. Reviewer (2-3 mins): what is and is not established by the paper.
  5. Open discussion (the rest): TAs will select students at random to pose their previously submitted questions to the panel.

Homework hand-in: 5 points.  Programming and calculation exercises.

There are five homeworks, jupyter notebooks to be worked through by students individually.  In this age of LLM assistance, we will not be scoring your homework code in detail.  Instead, each completed homework you hand in on Canvas earns 1 point, for a total of 5.

There is no deadline on this and no late penalty.  Hand a homework in whenever it is done, at any point in the semester — including before it is assigned, if you want to work ahead.  The dates in the schedule below are when each homework is meant to be done, because that is what the quizzes are timed against, but the point does not depend on them.

Homework quizzes: 50 points.  Five short quizzes, 10 points each, taken in class.

There is one homework quiz per homework assignment, and each quiz is given in class on the day that homework is due (the dates are in the schedule below).  Those days are deliberately days without a student paper presentation, so that the class is not carrying two things at once.  Each quiz tests your knowledge of the methods and concepts in that homework.  The quizzes are designed to be much easier for you if you have actually worked through and understood the homework yourself, so the best preparation for a quiz is to do the homework without leaning on an LLM to do the thinking for you.  This is where the real incentive to keep up with the homework lies: handing the homework in is worth 1 point, but its quiz is worth 10.

On each of those five days we will open the class with about ten minutes of informal discussion of the homework, before the quiz.  Bring your questions.  If that discussion happens to give away the answer to something on the quiz, so be it — that is a reward for being in the room and paying attention.

Quizzes must be taken in person, in class on their scheduled day, to earn points.  There is no remote or online version.

Each quiz is worth 10 points, and whether a quiz counts is decided as follows:

  1. A quiz that would pull your grade down is dropped — out of the course total as well as out of your points — so a quiz that goes badly does not count against you.
  2. A quiz you skip counts as a zero, and that zero stays in the total.
  3. Coming to a make-up drops the missed quiz instead, the same way a low quiz you sat would be dropped.

The reason for scoring quizzes this way is that we would much rather you came to class than stayed away worried about a quiz.  Come and take it.

For example: if you sit all five quizzes and they all help you, your grade is out of the full 205.  If two of them would drag you down, those two are dropped and your grade is out of 185.  If you sat none of them but came to all five make-up discussions, all fifty quiz points would drop out and your grade would be computed out of 155.

Make-ups run for 14 days after the quiz.  A make-up begins with a second sitting of the quiz, and is then graded together with a short discussion of that homework with a TA, rather than marked cold — so if there is something you did not follow, that conversation is the place to sort it out.  Come to office hours or arrange a time with a TA.  After 14 days the make-up closes and the zero stands.  If you know in advance that you will miss a quiz, tell us before the date rather than after.

Midterm exam: 50 points.  A written, closed-book exam about foundational methods, held in class.  The starred items in the concept list below are the ones that may appear on it.

Final Project: 50 points.  A blog/webpage report and presentation about a research paper that you choose. Done by groups of 2 students (3 with permission).  Similar to the paper-reading roleplaying exercise, except that you choose the papers, you play all the roles, and instead of putting together slides, you will assemble an illustrated blog with all your analysis.  Optionally, your project may include a programming demonstration or a research extension of the concepts in the paper that you choose.  The measure of the work is whether a reader finishes it understanding the paper better.

 

Homework has no deadline; the quizzes do

There is no late penalty on homework and there are no late days to keep track of.  Hand a homework in whenever it is done, at any time during the semester, and it earns its point.

The catch is that the quizzes do not move.  Each homework quiz is given in class on its scheduled day whether or not you have finished the homework.  The quizzes are worth 50 points and the homework hand-ins are worth 5, so the reason to do a homework on time is not the point — it is that the quiz a few days later is easy if you have done the work yourself and hard if you have not.

 

Collaboration Policy

Collaboration is allowed, but you should think about the problems yourself before discussing them with others.  When you do seek out help, we strongly advise you to find a fellow classmate to talk with and work together rather than copying an answer. You will all learn much more by thinking collaboratively and explaining ideas to one another.  When you collaborate, you must acknowledge your collaborators by listing them explicitly.
